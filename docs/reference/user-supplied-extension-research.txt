Beyond the core Sun/Earth visibility and the Life-Support/gravity models we've already discussed, deep research into recent lunar mission planning literature reveals several high-impact physical and operational constraints that remain unaddressed by standard site-selection tools.

Integrating even a subset of these would push SiteLight AI from a visualization tool into a genuine mission-architecting engine.

🌡️ 1. Site-Specific Thermal & Dust-Degraded Radiator Modeling
Current tools often treat temperature as a simple global metric. However, thermal survival at the South Pole is highly site-specific and heavily degraded by dust.

The Gap: Flat-ground thermal models fail to capture the extreme hot and cold environments created by local slopes, crater rims, and terrain occlusion. Furthermore, dust deposition on radiator surfaces significantly degrades their performance by increasing the sink temperature (reducing heat rejection capability), which can lead to higher daytime temperatures and longer nighttime survival durations.

How to Add It: Introduce a "Thermal Penalty" metric. For each site, calculate a dust-degraded radiator efficiency based on regional regolith composition. This becomes a mass penalty (larger radiators required) that feeds directly into your LCSS model, proving that a site with "good" illumination might still fail because its dust environment requires prohibitively heavy thermal control systems.

💨 2. Plume-Surface Interaction (PSI) & Ejecta Hazards
When a lander touches down, its rocket plume blasts the surface, creating high-velocity ejecta and lofted dust that can damage the vehicle or nearby assets.

The Gap: Landing site selection must account for the risk of "sand-blasted ejecta" and the time it takes for lofted dust to settle (which can be prolonged due to low gravity and electrostatics). This directly threatens EVA (Extravehicular Activity) operations and hardware.

How to Add It: Add a "PSI Risk" layer. Using published models, flag sites with high probabilities of severe ejecta damage based on local regolith composition. This can generate a "EVA Safe Waiting Time" (e.g., "Wait 48 hours post-landing for dust to settle before first moonwalk").

📡 3. Non-Line-of-Sight (NLOS) Comm Blackout & Relay Dependency
At the South Pole, Earth sits extremely low on the horizon, and terrain obstructions can cause complete communication blackouts lasting nearly two weeks.

The Gap: A simple line-of-sight check is insufficient. Radio frequency signals can reflect off rocky terrain, causing interference and data loss. For missions entering Permanently Shadowed Regions (PSRs), direct-to-Earth (DTE) links are completely precluded, requiring a surface communication network or orbital relay.

How to Add It: Implement a "Comm Blackout / Relay Dependency" flag. If a site (or a rover traversing from it) experiences a DTE blackout greater than a critical threshold (e.g., 24 hours), the tool automatically flags that a relay asset (surface tower or orbiter) is mandatory, and adds the mass of that relay to the mission's LCSS budget.

🚙 4. Rover Traverse & Reachability Envelopes
A landing site is only as good as what you can reach from it. South Pole terrain features slopes up to 20 degrees, which risk tipping vehicles.

The Gap: Path planning for polar rovers is severely affected by rugged terrain, low solar angles, and limited communication links. Traverses must simultaneously satisfy slope limits, battery levels, and communication availability.

How to Add It: Add a "Reachability Envelope" overlay to the 3D map. Using a simplified traversal algorithm, calculate the maximum radius a rover can travel from the landing site and return safely, given its battery capacity, slope limits, and the requirement to maintain a comm link. This reveals if scientifically valuable PSRs are actually accessible.

☢️ 5. Topographic Radiation Shielding
Galactic Cosmic Rays (GCR) and Solar Energetic Particles (SEP) are constant threats. While regolith can be used for shielding (requiring several meters of thickness), the local topography itself provides natural shielding.

The Gap: Recent studies show that dose differences of several tens of percent can occur at certain locations compared to a uniform flat model, due to the neutron shielding effects of surrounding terrain.

How to Add It: Introduce a "Natural Radiation Shielding" score. Calculate how much of the sky is blocked by surrounding hills and crater walls. A site tucked at the base of a massive ridge may have a lower radiation dose than a flat plateau, offering a safer habitat location.

🚨 6. The "Rescue Reachability" Constraint (The Unaddressed Safety Gap)
NASA's Office of Inspector General has identified a fundamental flaw in the Artemis architecture: there is no pre-positioned rescue capability at the South Pole.

The Gap: If a lander tips on rugged terrain, or a crew gets stranded, there is no backup vehicle. Current site-selection tools completely ignore this.

How to Add It: Create a "Rescue Viability" factor. Factor in the distance to the nearest traversable ridge or communication node. A site that marginally improves sunlight but is entirely unreachable by a hypothetical rescue asset within a critical survival window should be rejected on safety grounds.
