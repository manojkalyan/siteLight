The short answer is: Use real NASA data for the core challenge, and clearly-labeled published sample values for the LCSS extension. NASA does not hand you a curated dataset on a platter for this challenge—you are expected to pull from their open data archives. But the good news is that the exact data you need is publicly and freely available, and a prior submission (ARTEMIS) already proves it can be done in the hackathon window using real JPL ephemeris and NASA LOLA terrain data.

Here is the breakdown of where each piece of data comes from and the real-vs-sample decision for each.

🔭 Ephemeris Data (Sun and Earth Positions)
You need the positions of the Sun and Earth as seen from a lunar site. This is real, deterministic, and freely available—there is no reason to use sample data here.

Where to get it: Use the JPL DE421 or DE440 ephemeris kernels with a library like Skyfield (Python) or Sparrow (JavaScript). The ARTEMIS submission explicitly used JPL DE421 with IAU lunar rotation to compute Sun/Earth visibility with libration. The challenge brief itself suggests the JPL Horizons System as a legitimate tool for ephemeris calculation.

Real vs. Sample: Use real data. This is the scientific core of your tool. Mock ephemeris would undermine your credibility with judges, especially since a competitor already demonstrated real ephemeris.

🏔️ Topography Data (Terrain Horizon Profiles)
You need a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) to compute the azimuthal horizon profile—the terrain silhouette that determines when the Sun or Earth is actually blocked.

Where to get it: The NASA Planetary Data System (PDS) is the primary archive. Specifically:

LOLA DEMs: Available from the LOLA PDS Data Node (e.g., imbrium.mit.edu). The PGDA LOLA DEMs (5 m site DEMs) are explicitly listed as available resources.

QuickMap / Moon Trek: Both are web-based access points for LOLA and LROC data. The challenge brief itself names NASA QuickMap and Moon Trek as existing tools—you can use them to download DEM tiles for your curated sites.

Lunar Toolkit: An open-source Python package that can search, download, and process LROC imagery and LOLA DEMs, specifically including Artemis III regions and PGDA LOLA DEMs.

Real vs. Sample: Use real data for your curated sites. Download a LOLA DEM tile for each of your 5–6 sites and precompute the horizon profile in your build pipeline. For custom coordinates (if you implement them), you can fall back to the nearest available tile or a coarser global DEM—but label it explicitly in your provenance panel as lower-confidence.

💧 Water-Ice Concentration (for the LCSS Extension)
This is the data for your logistics mass penalty. This is where sample values are legitimate, because a full 3D resource model is beyond hackathon scope—but you must be honest about it.

Where to get real anchors:

LCROSS/Cabeus: The LCROSS impact team estimated south-polar regolith water ice at 5.6 ± 2.9 wt%. This is your highest-confidence anchor and should be the default for Cabeus-adjacent sites.

LRO Neutron Spectrometer / LEND: Provides hydrogen maps that can be converted to water-ice estimates. Permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) show elevated hydrogen, corresponding to ice at the ~1% level in the upper meter in some areas.

Artemis candidate regions: A published upper bound of ≤2.0 wt% is documented for Artemis candidate regions (their WEH is at most half of the LCROSS site).

Real vs. Sample: Use published sample values with clear provenance. You are not expected to process raw neutron-spectrometer data in 48 hours. Assign each curated site a documented value from the literature above, store the source and confidence flag in your data table, and display it in your provenance panel. The water-ice slider in your LCSS tab then becomes a feature, not a weakness: it lets the user explore how the logistics verdict changes as the resource assumption moves within the published uncertainty band.

💡 What NASA Actually “Provides” for Space Apps
The Space Apps program does not ship a pre-packaged dataset for each challenge. Instead, it provides access to NASA's open data ecosystem—PDS, Earthdata, and the challenge-specific “Resources” tab that opens on the event weekend. The official guidance to participants is to fetch and inspect a small real sample and use NASA's free and open data. This means your strategy of downloading LOLA DEMs and using published water-ice values is exactly aligned with how the event is designed.

⚠️ The One Rule: Never Hide Your Data Quality
Whatever mix of real and sample data you use, your provenance panel must make it visible. If a judge asks “is this real?” your answer should be: “The ephemeris and terrain are real NASA data; the water-ice concentration is a published screening value with a source and a slider so you can see how sensitive the conclusion is.” That level of honesty is itself a differentiator—and it protects you from the credibility risk of presenting a screening estimate as a design-grade number.
